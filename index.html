01 — Research
The Problem
Space colonization is technologically feasible. But economically, politically, and ethically — it is being built on the same systems of inequality that define life on Earth. This project investigates why, and proposes a path forward.
Technology
The Gap Is Closable
Current technology supports temporary bases. Permanent habitats need AI-driven self-repair, 3D printing, and nuclear power — all within reach, but expensive.
Economics
20 Years to Returns
Sandler & Schulze's model shows space colonies only become profitable after 20 years. Only sustained capital investment — accessible to very few — survives that timeline.
Politics
Geopolitics Drives Access
The Artemis Accords exclude India, China, and Russia. Space is being governed before it is colonized — and not everyone has a seat at the table.
Ethics
Who Gets a Seat?
Only a small portion of humanity can realistically migrate to space. Under current systems, that means the wealthy and the powerful go first.

04 — Policy
The Proposal
Based on original research and firsthand interviews, this project proposes three concrete policy changes to make space colonization equitable for all of humanity — not just the powerful few.
01
Mandatory Representation in Space Governance
Reform the Artemis Accords framework to require inclusion of non-signatory nations — including India, Brazil, and African Union members — in all decisions regarding resource extraction, territorial claims, and colonization governance. Space cannot be governed exclusively by the nations that got there first.
In Development
02
A COTS Model for Developing Nations
Establish an international equivalent of NASA's Commercial Orbital Transportation Services program — a capital injection model that funds private space companies in developing nations to reduce the cost threshold of entry. If NASA tripled efficiency by outsourcing to SpaceX, the same logic applies globally.
In Development
03
A 20-Year Sustained Investment Compact
Based on Sandler & Schulze's economic modeling, meaningful returns from space colonization only emerge after 20 years. A binding international investment compact — modeled on climate agreements — would commit signatories to sustained funding beyond electoral cycles, preventing the political volatility that has historically collapsed space programs.
