01 — Research
The Landscape
Space colonization is technologically within reach. What determines who gets there — and who shapes the governance of everything that follows — is a question of economics, politics, and competitive access. This project investigates how those dynamics work, and what policy can do about them.
Technology
The Hardware Exists
Kilopower reactors tested in 2018, AI-driven habitat repair, on-site 3D printing, self-healing materials — the technology for permanent colonization is real. The gap isn't hardware. It's sustained political and economic will to deploy it.
Economics
Competition Drives Efficiency
When NASA competed with Soviet spending, it produced Apollo. When it competed commercially through COTS, SpaceX cut per-kg delivery costs by 3×. Competition — not monopoly — is the engine of space economic efficiency.
Politics
Will Follows Competition
NASA's budget rose from 0.5% to 4.4% of GDP during the space race. After the race ended in 1975, it crashed — and successful mission counts dropped simultaneously. Political will doesn't exist in a vacuum. It requires a rival.
Access
The Race Starts Unequal
The Artemis Accords — governing lunar and Martian access — exclude Russia, China, and India. Space governance is being locked in before the full field of competitors has assembled. The framework being built now will determine access for a generation.

02 — Framework
Controlled Competition
The argument isn't about fairness. It's about incentive structures. Open competition with guardrails produces better outcomes for every nation — including the ones already ahead. Here's the case, built from the evidence.
01
Competition Produces the Best Outcomes — We Have the Proof
Apollo didn't happen because America wanted to go to the Moon. It happened because the Soviets were going. Two rivals pushing each other past what either could do alone produced the most extraordinary technological leap in human history. The lesson isn't that competition is nice — it's that competition works, and the data tracks it precisely.
NASA budget rose from 0.5% to 4.4% of US GDP during the Cold War. When the space race ended in 1975, budget and successful mission counts crashed together back to pre-Apollo levels — simultaneously. (Mosila 2023, Rosen et al. 2022)
Private capital had been building space infrastructure for 150 years before Apollo. The Cold War didn't create the space industry — it amplified what competitive private investment had already built. (MacDonald 2017)
COTS introduced competition into cargo delivery. SpaceX achieved $89K/kg vs NASA's own $272K/kg — same mission, same payload class, 3× efficiency. Competition-driven, not government-designed. (Weinzierl 2018)
In Apollo-era conditions — where competition forces genuinely new technology rather than optimization — the GDP spending multiplier hits 21× over 20 years. Today's incremental innovation produces only 10×. More real competition means larger returns. (Corrado et al. 2023)

02
Wealthy Nations Have Self-Interested Reasons to Want More Competitors
This is the part that gets missed. Funding developing-nation space programs isn't charity — it's strategy. New competitors create new pressure. New pressure forces genuine innovation, not optimization. More innovation pushes the spillover coefficient back toward Apollo-era levels. The investors collect on that difference. There is a direct financial incentive to grow the field.
Space investment creates STEM talent pipelines that spill over into every other sector. Engineers trained for space end up in semiconductors, biotech, materials science. The talent compounds regardless of where individuals land — India's ISRO investment catalyzed its entire tech sector. (Corrado et al. 2023)
Asteroid cobalt is 300× more concentrated than on Earth (6,000 vs 20 g/mt). Nickel is 1,162× more concentrated. Whoever controls space mining gains structural leverage over global manufacturing. Multiple competitors distribute that leverage — no single actor dominates. (Fleming et al. 2023)
A GDP spending shock in space returns 10× over 20+ years — larger than most traditional fiscal spending categories. Nations that invest in creating new competitors collect ROI on the ecosystem their investment builds. (Corrado et al. 2023)
Monopoly in space is a strategic risk, not a strategic advantage. One nation controlling orbital infrastructure and asteroid mining rights can lever geopolitical pressure on every other nation — including current allies. Open competition is strategically safer. (Harding 2012)
03
Uncontrolled Competition Produces the Worst Outcome — Guardrails Keep It Sustainable
The case for guardrails isn't about slowing competition — it's about keeping it from collapsing into either monopoly or conflict. Uncontrolled competition eventually tips toward one actor dominating and locking others out, or toward direct resource wars. Both scenarios destroy the competitive dynamic that makes space investment valuable in the first place.
Without competitive pressure, political will evaporates. The 1975 post-space-race collapse — budget and missions cratering simultaneously — shows that even well-funded programs can't sustain themselves without a rival. Guardrails that keep competition alive preserve the pressure. (Rosen et al. 2022)
Uncontrolled competition creates conflict risks: wars over asteroid mining rights, orbital infrastructure control, resource leverage between colonial powers. The damage could outweigh all economic benefits. (Kovic 2020)
COTS is the working model: government as investor and advisor, private sector as competitive executor, clear rules for what counts as winning. It produced 3× efficiency gains without a single conflict. The same structure scales internationally. (Iacomino & Ciccarelli 2018)

05 — Policy
The Proposals
Four concrete implementations of one framework: open competition with guardrails. Each proposal addresses a structural failure that prevents the competitive dynamics from producing optimal outcomes. None require nations to be altruistic — only self-interested in the right direction.
01
Competitive Governance Balance
Reform the Artemis Accords to require inclusion of non-signatory nations in governance decisions on resource extraction, territorial claims, and colonization frameworks. This isn't representation for its own sake — governance monopoly by one coalition produces the adversarial dynamic that eventually destabilizes the whole system. A rules-based framework that all major space actors participate in is the precondition for controlled competition rather than space colonialism.
Proposed
02
International COTS — Competition Seeding
Create a global equivalent of NASA's COTS model: capital injected into private space development in emerging economies, with government as investor and advisor rather than operator. New competitors push the Corrado spillover coefficient toward Apollo-era levels — the investing nations collect returns on the innovation their investment drives. This isn't foreign aid; it's building a competitive ecosystem that benefits the investors.
Proposed
03
20-Year Commitment Compact
A binding multilateral investment commitment that outlasts electoral cycles — modeled on WTO trade agreements. The Sandler-Schulze threshold is precise: BCR 0.4 at year 8, BCR 2.8 at year 20. The structural problem is a 4-year election cycle sitting inside a 20-year return horizon. A commitment compact changes the incentive structure: nations that bind themselves to the 20-year window collect the return. Nations that don't lose competitive position to those who did.
Proposed
04
Controlled Competition Framework
The umbrella mechanism: rules-based open access to space with multilateral guardrails preventing territorial monopolies, exclusive resource claims, and governance lock-out. WTO for space — competition-enabling rules that keep the race open and prevent early dominance from being used to exclude later entrants permanently. Free markets, transparent rules, no one nation with unchecked control. As Rep. Lorenz put it: guardrails so everyone knows what they're up against. The regulatory sandbox concept, applied at the international level.
